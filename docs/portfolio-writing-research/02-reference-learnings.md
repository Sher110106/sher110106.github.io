# Reference learnings

## Arjun Virk

Sources:

- `https://github.com/virkvarjun/arjunvirk.com.git`
- `https://arjunvirk.com`

What stands out:

- The homepage is simple and credibility-dense. It opens with a name, a bullet list of concrete roles/achievements, social links, a photo, and then work.
- Projects mix categories: research, teaching, writing, engineering, leadership. This makes the site feel like a public record of work, not only a portfolio grid.
- Every project has a date, category, image, title, and concise description. This makes scanning easy.
- Writing is treated as first-class work. "Machine Learning Bible" and "Robot See, Robot Do" sit alongside engineering/research projects rather than hidden in a separate blog silo.
- The repo uses structured content definitions for writing categories and chapters. This makes long writing navigable and scalable.
- The writing system supports guides and reflections differently. Guides get table-of-contents behavior; reflections can be smaller and more raw.
- The educational writing style explains technical topics with plain-language analogies, questions, definitions, diagrams, equations, tables, quizzes, and code blocks.
- The diagram architecture is extensive. It contains reusable interactive diagrams and a registry-like structure, which makes the writing feel custom and pedagogical.

What to borrow, not copy:

- Treat writing as a project surface, not an afterthought.
- Give every project a possible "story page" or "note page."
- Use diagrams to explain the actual system mechanics behind projects.
- Use content structure that can scale from short notes to long technical guides.
- Keep project descriptions concrete: what was built, where, when, and why it mattered.

What not to copy:

- Do not replicate the exact minimal white academic portfolio style. Sher's current site already has a strong terminal/pixel identity.
- Do not import the diagram-heavy ML guide format wholesale. For Sher, diagrams should explain healthcare AI, agents, timelines, OCR, and system pipelines.

## Alana Goyal

Sources:

- `https://github.com/alanagoyal/alanagoyal.git`
- `https://alanagoyal.com`

What stands out:

- The website is the product. It is a macOS-inspired desktop environment, not a normal personal homepage.
- Content lives inside familiar app metaphors: Notes, Messages, Finder, Photos, Music, Calendar, Terminal, Settings, Weather.
- The Notes app is both content system and interface. Public notes become the site content, while private notes are session-based.
- The site has playful depth: dock, menu bar, windows, app icons, notifications, lock/sleep/restart overlays, local state, swipe gestures.
- The README documents the architecture clearly, which is useful because the site itself is complex.
- The live Notes content has a stream-like feel: pinned notes, today/yesterday grouping, personal principles, reading list, bookmarks, favorite products, favorite sites, and an implementation note.
- The writing tone is casual, lowercase-heavy, direct, and personal. It reads like an actual note rather than polished marketing copy.

What to borrow, not copy:

- Pick one strong metaphor that fits Sher instead of making a generic blog.
- Content can feel alive if it is framed as notes, logs, traces, or artifacts.
- Public writing can include lightweight lists: principles, current reading, favorite tools, build notes, "how this works."
- A site can be memorable when the interaction model expresses the person's taste.

What not to copy:

- Do not build a full desktop OS clone unless the whole portfolio concept changes. It would be a huge implementation surface and not naturally tied to Sher's current portfolio.
- Do not copy Apple UI. Sher's existing language suggests terminal, system monitor, lab notebook, debug trace, clinical timeline, or agent console as better metaphors.

## Chloe Yan ferry page

Source: `https://www.chloeyan.me/ferry`

What stands out:

- The page is basically one strong interface idea: a ferry departure board.
- Visually, the board is a 3D object floating in a mostly empty room-like scene.
- The background is quiet, warm, and textured, with a horizontal division that makes the board feel like it is sitting in real space rather than on a flat webpage.
- The board is dark, slightly tilted in perspective, and rendered with depth/shadow.
- The red digital timestamp becomes the brightest accent.
- Tiny info/sound icons sit in opposite top corners, but the interface mostly gets out of the way.
- It uses real-world transit language: destination, gate, remarks, departing.
- The design is not trying to explain itself. The metaphor carries the page.
- The uniqueness comes from taking a mundane interface and making it the whole experience.

What to borrow:

- A writing section can use a specific information-display metaphor rather than a normal blog list.
- For Sher, possible equivalents: clinical timeline board, agent run log, lab notebook index, terminal file tree, experiment dashboard.

## rotating.parts

Source: `https://rotating.parts/`

What stands out:

- The site appears to be a grid of many electronic/mechanical parts, each linked to a DigiKey search.
- Visually, the page is almost all product cutouts on a white background.
- There is no visible frame, headline, navigation, or explanatory copy in the captured viewport.
- The objects have real product-photo specificity: connectors, fans, plugs, enclosures, pins, housings.
- The grid is loose and airy rather than boxed. Each item feels like it is floating on a catalog table.
- It is highly visual, repetitive, and conceptually simple.
- The entire site is memorable because it commits to one constraint.
- The content is not explained with paragraphs; the pattern itself is the concept.

What to borrow:

- A dense grid can work when each item has a strong visual identity.
- Sher's work could use a "parts bin" metaphor: projects as components, notes as schematics, diagrams as system pieces.

## Aidan Smith

Source: `https://aidanjs.com/`

What stands out:

- Firecrawl extracted very little text: title, homepage link, about/blog navigation, and metadata.
- The page is not visually sparse. The markdown is sparse, but the screenshot shows a loud graphic identity.
- The viewport is a saturated orange field filled with repeated illustrated eyes.
- The name "AIDAN SMITH" appears in very large white uppercase letters across the top left.
- The eyes form a strict repeated pattern, almost like wallpaper or a surveillance motif.
- The top-right nav appears as two larger eye shapes with green irises containing curved text for "about" and "blog."
- The page communicates through a single bold graphic system, not through content density.

What to borrow:

- A personal site can be recognizable from one custom graphic motif.
- Navigation can be integrated into the motif instead of placed in a standard nav bar.
- Repetition can become identity if the repeated object is specific enough.

## Lee Robinson

Sources:

- `https://leerob.com/`
- `https://leerob.com/writing`

What stands out:

- The homepage says exactly who he is in a few paragraphs: current role, previous role, years of coding/teaching, life mission, personal details, favorite writing, and contact paths.
- The writing page is radically plain: title, byline link, and a list of writing.
- The writing titles are clear and promise value. Examples include beliefs, stack, CSS, agents, compression, product engineers.
- The home copy mixes professional credibility with small personal details, which makes it feel human.

What to borrow:

- A simple writing index is still useful even if the homepage is visually richer.
- Writing titles should be specific and curiosity-producing.
- Personal details can make technical work easier to remember.

## okfrank

Sources:

- `https://www.okfrank.co/`
- user screenshot of okfrank page

What stands out:

- The about page is a short career narrative, not a resume table.
- It uses first-person paragraphs with clear transitions: current, prior, before that, past life, lived places, contact.
- Links are embedded naturally into the prose.
- The screenshot shows a warm off-white background, large comfortable type, underlined links, and a huge hand-drawn line illustration.
- The illustration adds personality without turning the page into a decorative card layout.

What to borrow:

- Write career/project context as a narrative arc.
- Use one memorable drawing or schematic as a visual anchor.
- Embedded links can make prose feel less resume-like.
