# Writing style notes

## What the references have in common

The strongest pages do not sound like generic portfolio copy. They sound like someone thinking in public.

Common traits:

- first-person voice,
- specific nouns,
- direct claims,
- concrete artifacts,
- short paragraphs,
- natural links inside prose,
- some personal texture,
- enough informality to feel human,
- enough structure to stay useful.

## Lee Robinson pattern

Lee's homepage is effective because it answers:

- who I am,
- what I do now,
- what I did before,
- how long I have been doing it,
- what my life's work is,
- what else I care about,
- where to read more.

Writing index pattern:

- no thumbnails,
- no abstracts,
- just a list of strong article titles.

Useful lesson:

Sher can have a visually richer homepage section while still keeping `/writing` simple and fast to scan.

## okfrank pattern

Frank's page reads like a compact career story:

- current role,
- major recent work,
- previous company and product idea,
- earlier platform work,
- first unusual job,
- cities lived in,
- contact.

The prose does not list everything. It selects a few signals and connects them.

Useful lesson:

Project writing should not read like:

> I built X using Y and Z.

It can read more like:

> The strange part of this project was that the hard problem was not extraction. It was continuity.

That kind of opener creates a reason to keep reading.

## Alana pattern

Alana's live notes are informal, lowercase, and list-driven. The effect is intimate because the content feels like a real personal knowledge base.

Useful formats:

- principles,
- bookmarks,
- favorite products,
- reading list,
- favorite websites,
- implementation notes,
- "how this works."

Useful lesson:

Sher's writing section can include more than polished essays:

- build notes,
- experiment notes,
- research logs,
- "things I learned building X",
- favorite papers/tools,
- short diagrams,
- project postmortems.

## Arjun pattern

Arjun's writing is structured for teaching:

- concepts are introduced from intuition first,
- math/definitions appear after motivation,
- diagrams are embedded as explanatory tools,
- quizzes/checks appear in long guides,
- writing categories separate guides from reflections.

Useful lesson:

Sher's deeper technical pieces should be explanatory, not merely descriptive. The articles should teach the reader something about the system.

## Syahdan screenshot pattern

The screenshot uses repeated "i..." lines:

- i design...
- i work...
- i help...
- i believe...
- i think...

This makes the page feel direct and authored.

Useful lesson:

- I write to remember what the build taught me.
- I care about systems that track change over time.
- I prefer notes that show the messy middle.
- I want projects to leave behind artifacts, not just demos.

## Good writing directions for Sher

### Voice

The voice should be:

- curious,
- precise,
- slightly informal,
- systems-minded,
- honest about uncertainty,
- more "here is what surprised me" than "here is why this is impressive."

### Article openers

Strong opener types:

- The surprising problem: "The hard part was not extraction. It was continuity."
- The build tension: "The agent could click the page, but it still did not know what failure looked like."
- The artifact first: "This started as two messy PDFs and a student asking what they were missing."
- The human reason: "A curriculum gap sounds administrative until you are the student stuck between systems."

### Article structure

Useful default structure:

1. What started it.
2. What was confusing.
3. What I built.
4. What changed after testing.
5. What I would rebuild.
6. Links, diagrams, repo, screenshots.

### Titles

Avoid generic titles:

- "Fact Graph Project"
- "Bugzer Blog"
- "Curriculum Analyzer"

Prefer titles with tension:

- "Teaching a clinical system to remember"
- "Debugging websites by watching them fail"
- "Finding the missing concepts between two courses"
- "When a nodule becomes a lesion"
- "What an agent sees before it explains a bug"
